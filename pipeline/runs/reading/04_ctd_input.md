# Misconceptions Knowledge Base

## [Craft & Structure] Author's Purpose
**Misconception:** A text can only have one author's purpose (e.g., it is either to inform OR to persuade, never both).
**Correct:** Complex texts, especially in nursing and healthcare, often have a primary and secondary purpose. A passage may primarily inform the reader about the pathophysiology of diabetes but secondarily persuade the reader to adopt healthier lifestyle habits. The TEAS 7 often asks for the *primary* purpose, requiring students to weigh the emphasis of the passage.
**Common wrong answer:** Selecting "to entertain" because a medical passage includes a brief, humorous anecdote to engage the reader, missing the heavy informational bulk.
**Why students err:** The "PIE" (Persuade, Inform, Entertain) acronym taught in early education creates a false dichotomy in students' minds, framing purposes as mutually exclusive.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 passages frequently blend purposes, testing a student's ability to evaluate the *overall* weight of the text.
**Source:** ATI TEAS 7 Study Manual (2022); Archer Review TEAS Prep.

## [Craft & Structure] Figurative Language
**Misconception:** Figurative language is only used in literature and poetry, not in scientific or medical texts.
**Correct:** Figurative language is a pervasive cognitive tool used in science and medicine to explain abstract or invisible processes. Metaphors (e.g., "immune system defense"), similes (e.g., "cell division is like a choreographed dance"), and personification are essential to scientific communication and patient education.
**Common wrong answer:** Assuming a description of a disease is purely literal and failing to identify the metaphorical comparison being made to explain a mechanism.
**Why students err:** Curriculum tracking often segregates "literary devices" into English classes and "facts" into science classes, creating artificial boundaries in students' schema.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 science-adjacent reading passages frequently use analogies and metaphors to describe pathophysiology or anatomy.
**Source:** Archer Review TEAS 7 Overview; Lakoff & Johnson, "Metaphors We Live By" (1980).

## [Craft & Structure] Point of View
**Misconception:** Point of view refers only to the grammatical person (first, second, or third person) used in the passage.
**Correct:** While grammatical person is one component, "point of view" in advanced reading comprehension also encompasses the author's rhetorical stance, bias, perspective, or angle on the topic. A third-person passage can still have a strong point of view (e.g., a strong bias in favor of universal healthcare).
**Common wrong answer:** Identifying the point of view simply as "third-person omniscient" when the question is actually asking about the author's *perspective or bias* regarding a medical controversy.
**Why students err:** Elementary literary instruction over-indexes on grammatical perspective, leaving students unprepared for questions about rhetorical stance or ideological point of view.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 reading frequently features persuasive or argumentative healthcare policy passages where identifying the author's bias (point of view) is critical.
**Source:** Khan Academy Reading Comprehension; Common Core State Standards for ELA.

## [Craft & Structure] Text Structure & Transitions
**Misconception:** Transition words always reliably indicate a single, specific text structure (e.g., "similarly" always means compare/contrast).
**Correct:** While transition words are strong clues, they must be analyzed in context. "Similarly" might indicate a comparison, but it could also signal an analogy within a cause-effect passage. Furthermore, the absence of transition words does not mean a structure isn't present; relationships can be implied through semantic parallels.
**Common wrong answer:** Identifying a passage as Compare/Contrast simply because the word "however" appears once, ignoring that 90% of the passage is a sequential Problem/Solution structure.
**Why students err:** Rote memorization of signal word lists without instruction on how those words function within broader rhetorical contexts.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 answer choices often include structural distractors based on single signal words, testing holistic structural analysis over keyword hunting.
**Source:** ATI TEAS 7 Study Manual (2022); Ackerman, "Reading for Understanding" (1991).

## [Craft & Structure] Vocabulary in Context
**Misconception:** To find the meaning of a word in context, you should rely on your prior knowledge of the word's dictionary definition.
**Correct:** Vocabulary-in-context questions on the TEAS 7 frequently test common words used in *atypical* ways, or technical jargon surrounded by contextual clues. Students must use syntax, surrounding antonyms/synonyms, and the logical flow of the sentence to determine the specific meaning intended by the author in that exact instance.
**Common wrong answer:** Selecting the most common dictionary definition of a word, even if it makes the sentence nonsensical.
**Why students err:** Automatic word recognition bypasses the cognitive effort required to analyze context. When students "know" a word, they rarely stop to read how it's functioning in a specific clause.
**TEAS relevance:** ATI heavily features "words in context" that are multisyllabic but have varied meanings depending on usage (e.g., "obscure," "strain," "yield").
**Source:** Nagy, "Teaching Vocabulary to Improve Reading Comprehension" (1988).

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Correlation vs Causation
**Misconception:** Correlation (when two variables increase or decrease together) proves that one variable caused the other.
**Correct:** Correlation indicates a relationship or pattern between two variables, but it does not prove causation. The relationship could be coincidental, or a third, unmeasured variable (a confounding variable) could be causing both. Establishing causation requires controlled experimental design.
**Common wrong answer:** Assuming that because a study showed that higher nurse staffing levels correlate with lower patient mortality, the staffing levels *caused* the lower mortality, ignoring other factors like hospital funding levels.
**Why students err:** The human brain is wired to detect patterns and instinctively assign causal narratives to coinciding events (patternicity).
**TEAS relevance:** The False Cause (Post Hoc) fallacy is heavily tested in TEAS 7 reading passages that present observational studies or public health statistics.
**Source:** ATI TEAS 7 Study Manual (2022); Stanovich, "How to Think Straight About Psychology" (2007).

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Dual-Passage Comparison
**Misconception:** When comparing two passages on the same topic, if they disagree, one must be completely right and the other completely wrong.
**Correct:** Dual passages often present complementary or partially overlapping perspectives. Passage A might focus on the short-term efficacy of a treatment, while Passage B focuses on its long-term side effects. Both can be factually accurate but emphasize different variables or criteria for evaluation.
**Common wrong answer:** Stating that Passage B completely disproves Passage A, when Passage B actually just provides additional, contrasting nuances.
**Why students err:** Dichotomous thinking (black-and-white reasoning) drives students to force a "winner/loser" dynamic onto complex, multi-faceted informational texts.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 dual-passage sets frequently use this "different criteria" trap to test nuanced synthesis.
**Source:** Archer Review TEAS 7 Overview; ACT Reading Dual Passage Research.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Evaluating Evidence
**Misconception:** If an argument includes statistics or numerical data, it is automatically strong and credible.
**Correct:** Data is only as strong as the methodology behind it. Statistics can be cherry-picked, taken out of context, derived from a biased sample size, or irrelevant to the actual claim being made. A strong argument requires *relevant, representative* evidence, not just the presence of numbers.
**Common wrong answer:** Choosing the argument with the most statistics as the most credible, even if the statistics are from an anonymous blog.
**Why students err:** The "authority heuristic" causes students to view quantitative data as inherently more objective and trustworthy than qualitative descriptions.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 frequently pairs a logically sound argument without statistics against a poorly reasoned argument full of random numbers to test this exact distinction.
**Source:** Metzger, "Making Sense of Credibility on the Web" (2007); ATI TEAS 7 Study Manual.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Logical Fallacies
**Misconception:** A logical fallacy means the conclusion of the argument is factually false.
**Correct:** A logical fallacy indicates a flaw in the *reasoning process* or structure of the argument, not necessarily the truth value of the conclusion. One can arrive at a true conclusion using terrible logic (e.g., "Ice cream sales increase in summer; shark attacks increase in summer; therefore, ice cream causes shark attacks"). The conclusion (shark attacks increase) is true, but the causal link is fallacious.
**Common wrong answer:** Concluding that a claim is false simply because the author used a logical fallacy to support it.
**Why students err:** Students conflate the validity of an argument (logical structure) with the soundness/truth of its conclusion.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 questions ask students to identify the *flaw in the reasoning*, not to determine if the ultimate topic is true or false.
**Source:** Toulmin, "The Uses of Argument" (1958); Khan Academy Logical Reasoning.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Source Bias & Credibility
**Misconception:** If a source has a clear bias, it cannot contain any factual information or valid data.
**Correct:** Bias refers to a predisposition or favoritism that skews interpretation. A highly biased source (e.g., a pharmaceutical manufacturer's website) can still state 100% factual data (e.g., the chemical formula of a drug). Credibility requires evaluating *why* the data is presented and *what* is omitted, not dismissing the text entirely.
**Common wrong answer:** Evaluating a biased source as "completely useless" rather than identifying *how* the bias impacts the specific argument being made.
**Why students err:** Students are taught "bias is bad" without being taught the metacognitive skills to separate verifiable facts from biased interpretations within the same text.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 answer choices often include "The source is completely invalid because it is biased" as a distractor, whereas the correct answer is more specific (e.g., "The source may overstate the drug's benefits due to financial conflict of interest").
**Source:** Wineburg, "Historical Thinking and Other Unnatural Acts" (2001).

## [Key Ideas & Details] Fact vs Opinion
**Misconception:** Opinions are false statements, while facts are true statements.
**Correct:** A fact is a statement that can be objectively verified or proven true/false through evidence, regardless of personal feelings. An opinion is a statement of belief, judgment, or feeling that cannot be objectively proven. An opinion can be based on facts, and a fact can be completely false (e.g., "The patient's temperature is 98.6°F" is a fact, even if the thermometer reads 104°F).
**Common wrong answer:** Identifying a highly biased, emotionally charged opinion as a "false fact" rather than an opinion.
**Why students err:** Conflation of truth-value with verifiability. Students associate things they disagree with (or extreme statements) as "false" rather than subjective.
**TEAS relevance:** Distinguishing fact from opinion is a core TEAS 7 standard, frequently tested using healthcare policy debates or editorial passages.
**Source:** ATI TEAS 7 Study Manual (2022).

## [Key Ideas & Details] Inference
**Misconception:** Making an inference means making an educated guess based mostly on personal background knowledge.
**Correct:** An inference in standardized reading is a logical conclusion drawn *strictly* from the evidence provided in the text combined with basic reasoning. While background knowledge activates the process, the conclusion must be directly tethered to textual clues; if the text doesn't support it, it is not a valid inference.
**Common wrong answer:** An answer choice that applies a real-world medical fact to the passage that the passage never actually implied.
**Why students err:** In everyday life, "inferring" is synonymous with guessing or assuming. Nursing students, who possess growing medical knowledge, particularly struggle to silo their external knowledge from the text.
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 intentionally tests healthcare topics where students have prior knowledge to see if they can strictly adhere to the provided text.
**Source:** Khan Academy Reading Comprehension strategies; National Reading Panel Report (2000).

## [Key Ideas & Details] Main Idea Identification
**Misconception:** The main idea of a paragraph is always explicitly stated in the very first sentence.
**Correct:** While paragraphs often begin with a topic sentence, TEAS 7 passages frequently use introductory hooks, rhetorical questions, or anecdotal evidence before stating the main idea. The main idea may appear in the middle or end, or it may be implied entirely, requiring the reader to synthesize the supporting details into a single overarching claim.
**Common wrong answer:** Selecting an introductory anecdote or background detail from the first sentence.
**Why students err:** High school writing pedagogy heavily emphasizes the "deductive" paragraph structure (topic sentence first). Students overgeneralize this rule as a universal reading comprehension heuristic.
**TEAS relevance:** ATI frequently tests passages with narrative leads or historical context before introducing the core scientific or informational concept.
**Source:** ATI TEAS 7 Study Manual (2022); Duke & Pearson, "Effective Practices for Developing Reading Comprehension" (2002).

## [Key Ideas & Details] Summarization
**Misconception:** Summarizing a passage means copying the most important sentence or just cutting off the ending.
**Correct:** Summarizing is an active cognitive process that requires deleting trivial details, collapsing redundant information, and constructing macro-propositions (superordinate statements) that capture the overarching message, often in the student's own words.
**Common wrong answer:** Selecting a sentence that contains a key vocabulary word but only represents a minor supporting detail.
**Why students err:** Students lack training in macro-rule application (specifically generalization and construction) and default to "sentence lifting."
**TEAS relevance:** TEAS 7 summary questions require selecting the option that encompasses the entire scope of the passage without getting bogged down in specific examples.
**Source:** Brown & Day, "Macrorules of Summarizing Texts" (1983).

## [Key Ideas & Details] Textual Support
**Misconception:** If an answer choice is factually correct in the real world, it is the correct answer to a reading comprehension question.
**Correct:** Correct answers must be both factually accurate *and* supported by the passage. A factually true statement that introduces new information, expands beyond the author's scope, or focuses on an irrelevant detail is a distractor.
**Common wrong answer:** A statement that is scientifically true (e.g., "The heart pumps oxygenated blood to the body") but is not the focus or detail requested by the question prompt.
**Why students err:** Cognitive overload causes students to rely on recognition heuristics ("I know this is true, so it must be the answer") rather than evaluating textual support.
**TEAS relevance:** This is arguably the most common trap on the TEAS 7 Reading section, particularly in anatomy and physiology passages.
**Source:** Archer Review TEAS 7 Overview; Haladyna, Downing, & Rodriguez, "A Taxonomy of Multiple-Choice Item-Writing Rules" (2002).

# High-Yield Q&A

## [Craft & Structure] Passage: "The human body's immune response is a breathtaking symphony. When a pathogen breaches the perimeter, the macrophages act as the first violins, sounding the initial alarm. The T-cells then join the chorus, harmonizing to destroy the infected cells." What type of figurative language is primarily used in this passage?
**Answer:** Metaphor
**Explanation:** The author directly compares the immune system to a symphony and specific cells to musicians without using "like" or "as" (which would indicate a simile). Personification is secondary here.

## [Craft & Structure] What is the author's primary purpose in the immune response passage?
**Answer:** To explain a complex biological process using an accessible comparison.
**Explanation:** The purpose is informative/explanatory. While it uses figurative language (which might feel entertaining), the goal is to help the reader understand how the immune system works.

## [Craft & Structure] Passage: "Many believe that alternative medicines are completely benign. *However*, clinical trials frequently demonstrate adverse interactions between herbal supplements and prescription medications." In this context, what does the word "benign" mean?
**Answer:** Harmless
**Explanation:** The transition "However" signals a contrast. If clinical trials show "adverse interactions" (harm), the opposing belief must be that they are harmless. Students with medical knowledge might incorrectly choose "non-cancerous," which is a medical definition of benign, but doesn't fit this context.

## [Craft & Structure] Which of the following signal words in a passage would most strongly indicate a Cause/Effect organizational structure?
**Answer:** Consequently
**Explanation:** "Consequently" denotes a result or effect stemming from a prior cause. "Similarly" (compare), "First" (sequence), and "For instance" (illustration) signal other structures.

## [Craft & Structure] Passage: "The shift from paper charting to digital EHRs was not without growing pains. Initially, nurses found the software interfaces cumbersome, leading to slower documentation times. Yet, after six months of dedicated training, documentation speed improved by 20%, and error rates dropped significantly." What is the overall text structure of this paragraph?
**Answer:** Problem-Solution
**Explanation:** The passage presents a problem (growing pains, cumbersome software, slower times) and the solution that resolved it (six months of training), followed by the positive results. It is not strictly cause-effect because the focus is on overcoming an obstacle.

## [Craft & Structure] What is the author's point of view regarding EHRs in the passage about the shift from paper charting to digital EHRs?
**Answer:** The author believes that despite initial difficulties, EHRs are ultimately beneficial when proper training is provided.
**Explanation:** The author acknowledges the negative ("growing pains") but emphasizes the positive outcome ("error rates dropped significantly"), showing a nuanced, optimistic stance.

## [Craft & Structure] Passage: "Antibiotics are ineffective against viruses. Viruses replicate by hijacking host cells, a mechanism entirely distinct from bacterial reproduction. Therefore, prescribing antibiotics for a viral infection is not only futile but contributes to antibiotic resistance." What is the tone of this passage?
**Answer:** Direct and cautionary
**Explanation:** The author uses definitive language ("ineffective," "entirely distinct," "not only futile") to warn the reader against a specific dangerous action, making the tone direct and cautionary. It is not emotional or sympathetic.

## [Craft & Structure] In the antibiotic passage, the phrase "entirely distinct" is used to describe the relationship between viral replication and bacterial reproduction. This phrase functions primarily to:
**Answer:** Emphasize the difference between the two processes to justify why the same treatment won't work.
**Explanation:** The author isn't just comparing for the sake of comparing; the structural purpose of highlighting this distinction is to logically support the claim that antibiotics don't work on viruses.

## [Craft & Structure] Which of the following best describes the organizational pattern of a passage that begins with a broad definition of epidemiology, narrows down to a specific outbreak, and then details the step-by-step investigation of that outbreak?
**Answer:** General to Specific
**Explanation:** Moving from a broad concept (definition) to a narrow focus (specific outbreak) is the hallmark of general-to-specific (or deductive) organization.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Passage: "After the new hospital wing opened, the rate of patient falls decreased by 15%. Therefore, the new hospital wing prevents patient falls." Which of the following logical fallacies is present in this argument?
**Answer:** False cause (assuming correlation implies causation)
**Explanation:** The passage assumes that because the wing opening and the fall rate decrease happened at the same time, one caused the other. It ignores confounding variables (e.g., perhaps a new fall-risk assessment protocol was implemented at the exact same time).

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Passage: "We must either completely ban the use of physical restraints in all healthcare facilities, or we are actively participating in patient abuse." This statement is an example of which logical fallacy?
**Answer:** False dilemma
**Explanation:** The author presents only two extreme options, ignoring the middle ground (e.g., strictly regulating restraints, using them only as a last resort with specific documentation), which is standard in nursing practice.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] A student is researching the efficacy of a new diet pill. They find two sources. Source A is a peer-reviewed article in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* (JAMA) detailing a double-blind study with 5,000 participants. Source B is a blog post by a celebrity who lost weight while taking the pill. Which source is more credible and why?
**Answer:** Source A, because it utilizes a large sample size, a rigorous methodology, and is published in a peer-reviewed medical journal.
**Explanation:** Credibility hinges on expertise, methodology, and peer review. Source B relies on anecdotal evidence and lacks medical expertise or controls.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Passage A argues that 12-hour nursing shifts improve patient continuity of care. Passage B argues that 12-hour shifts lead to nurse fatigue and increased medical errors. How do the passages relate to one another?
**Answer:** They present opposing views on the same topic, focusing on different criteria to evaluate the shift length.
**Explanation:** Passage A focuses on continuity of care (a benefit), while Passage B focuses on fatigue/errors (a drawback). They do not contradict each other's facts; they emphasize different consequences.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Based on Passage A and Passage B about 12-hour nursing shifts, which of the following statements represents a valid synthesis of both passages?
**Answer:** While 12-hour shifts offer the advantage of continuous patient care, they simultaneously introduce the risk of fatigue-related errors.
**Explanation:** Synthesis requires combining elements of both texts accurately without favoring one or misrepresenting their claims. This option successfully merges the core thesis of both.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Passage: "A survey of 50 people at a local gym found that 90% prefer working out in the morning. Therefore, 90% of all adults prefer morning workouts." What is the primary weakness of this argument?
**Answer:** The sample size is too small and not representative of the general adult population.
**Explanation:** The argument generalizes a highly specific, biased sample (gym-goers who happened to be there in the morning) to the entire population. This is a flaw in sampling/representation.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] A passage includes the statement: "Water is essential for human survival; therefore, drinking exactly 8 glasses of water a day is the only way to stay healthy." Which of the following best describes the author's use of evidence?
**Answer:** The author uses a generally accepted fact to support a highly specific, unsupported claim.
**Explanation:** The premise (water is essential) is true, but it does not logically lead to the conclusion (exactly 8 glasses is the *only* way). The evidence is insufficient and disconnected from the claim.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Passage: "Dr. Smith, a leading cardiologist, claims that Chocolate Cheerios are the optimal breakfast for heart health. However, Dr. Smith is a paid consultant for the cereal's manufacturer." How does this information affect the credibility of Dr. Smith's claim?
**Answer:** It introduces a conflict of interest, suggesting the claim may be financially motivated rather than scientifically objective.
**Explanation:** It does not prove the cereal is bad, but it significantly weakens the trustworthiness of the source due to a clear financial bias.

## [Integration of Knowledge & Ideas] Which of the following is the most effective way to evaluate whether a website is a reliable source for nursing research?
**Answer:** Checking if the site is affiliated with a recognized medical institution, cites peer-reviewed research, and clearly states its authorship and credentials.
**Explanation:** A holistic evaluation of authorship, sponsorship, and evidence citation is the gold standard for source credibility. Checking the site's visual design or popularity are irrelevant metrics.

## [Key Ideas & Details] Passage: "Despite the widespread adoption of electronic health records (EHRs), many nurses report significant burnout due to excessive screen time. A recent study found that nurses spend up to 35% of their shift interacting with EHR interfaces rather than providing direct patient care. Consequently, some hospitals are piloting the use of medical scribes to alleviate this documentation burden." What is the main idea of this passage?
**Answer:** The implementation of EHRs has unintended negative consequences for nurses that some hospitals are attempting to mitigate.
**Explanation:** The main idea synthesizes the problem introduced (EHR adoption leading to burnout/screen time) with the solution presented at the end (hospitals using scribes). Distractors might focus solely on the 35% statistic (supporting detail) or the definition of EHRs (background information).

## [Key Ideas & Details] Based on the same passage about EHRs and nurse burnout, which of the following can be logically inferred?
**Answer:** Prior to the adoption of EHRs, nurses likely spent a smaller percentage of their shifts on documentation.
**Explanation:** The passage states EHRs cause "excessive screen time" taking up "35% of their shift," implying a contrast with a previous state. You cannot infer that scribes will solve burnout entirely (going too far) or that EHRs are entirely bad (overgeneralizing).

## [Key Ideas & Details] Which of the following details from the EHR passage best supports the claim that EHRs contribute to nurse burnout?
**Answer:** Nurses spend up to 35% of their shift interacting with EHR interfaces rather than providing direct patient care.
**Explanation:** This provides the quantitative evidence linking the EHRs to a loss of direct patient care, which is framed as the source of burnout.

## [Key Ideas & Details] Passage: "The heart is a muscular organ roughly the size of a closed fist. It is situated between the lungs in the mediastinum." Which of the following is true based *only* on the passage?
**Answer:** The heart is located in a specific anatomical region between the lungs.
**Explanation:** This is a direct paraphrase. A distractor like "The heart pumps blood throughout the body" is factually true in the real world but is *not* stated in the provided text, testing the "true but unsupported" trap.

## [Key Ideas & Details] Passage: "Handwashing remains the cornerstone of infection control. It is simple, cost-effective, and saves lives. Every healthcare worker must comply with hand hygiene protocols." Which of the following is an opinion expressed in the passage?
**Answer:** Handwashing is the cornerstone of infection control.
**Explanation:** While highly accepted in medicine, calling something a "cornerstone" is a metaphor expressing a value judgment or belief, not an empirically verifiable statement in this context. "It is simple" and "saves lives" are also bordering on opinion/evaluation in this phrasing, but "cornerstone" is the strongest subjective metaphor.

## [Key Ideas & Details] Which of the following best summarizes the passage about handwashing?
**Answer:** Handwashing is a vital, simple practice that is mandatory for all healthcare personnel to control infections.
**Explanation:** A good summary captures the "what" (handwashing), "why" (infection control/saves lives), and "who" (healthcare workers) without repeating the exact phrasing.

## [Key Ideas & Details] Passage: "To properly don a sterile gown, the nurse must first wash their hands. Next, they pick up the gown from the inside neckline, allowing it to unfold without touching non-sterile surfaces. The arms are then inserted into the sleeves, and a circulating nurse ties the back." What conclusion can be drawn from this passage?
**Answer:** Donning a sterile gown requires a specific sequence of actions to maintain sterility.
**Explanation:** The passage outlines a chronological, highly specific process. The logical conclusion is that the sequence matters for the outcome (sterility). You cannot conclude that doing it out of order will immediately infect a patient (too extreme).

## [Key Ideas & Details] According to the passage on donning a sterile gown, what must occur immediately before the nurse inserts their arms into the sleeves?
**Answer:** The gown must be allowed to unfold without touching non-sterile surfaces.
**Explanation:** This is a literal comprehension question testing chronological order. The keywords "Next" and "then" dictate the sequence.

## [Key Ideas & Details] Passage: "Patient compliance with medication regimens is a multifaceted issue. Factors include financial constraints, side effects, and cognitive decline." Which of the following is a fact in this passage?
**Answer:** Patient compliance is influenced by multiple factors.
**Explanation:** This is a verifiable, objective statement based on the text's categorization. "Compliance is the most important issue in healthcare" would be an opinion.

## Scoring Rubric
For each question, assign:
- **3 points:** Direct hit — KB has an exact misconception match or Q&A that directly addresses this
- **2 points:** Adjacent — KB covers a related concept that partially answers the question
- **1 point:** Partial — KB has general info about the topic area but nothing specific enough
- **0 points:** Gap — KB has nothing relevant to this question
